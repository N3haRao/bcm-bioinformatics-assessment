        paired t-test : t = -0.6369, p = 0.5318
        Wilcoxon      : W = 72.0000, p = 0.5565
        Verdict: NOT SIGNIFICANT. This ignores row/column identity
        entirely and is dominated by the fact that M2's grand total
        (471) is simply bigger than M1's (417). It is comparing
        'cell number k of M1' to 'cell number k of M2' as if they
        were a matched pair of the same quantity, which they are not.

  (b) Sum away the 5 columns, one chi-square on the 4 row totals:
        chi2 = 103.7052, dof = 3, p = 2.481e-22
        Verdict: highly significant, roughly the right ballpark, but
        this throws away every column-level fact worth knowing: it

        dominate the result rather than weighing each column as one
        independent piece of evidence.

Section 3: combining the 5 independent columns into one verdict
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Fisher's combined probability test (requires independent p-values,
  which is exactly what independent columns give us):
    chi2 = 131.0674, dof = 10, p = 2.829e-23

  Stouffer's Z method (cross-check, less sensitive to one dominant
  column than Fisher's method):
    unweighted   Z = 9.0162, p = 9.738e-20
    weighted by column sample size   Z = 9.0276, p = 8.773e-20

  Harmonic mean p-value (robust combined figure, no direct null):
    p_harmonic = 3.583e-14

  Generalized Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test (columns as independent
  strata, pools the actual association rather than just the p-values,
  validated against statsmodels' 2x2xK implementation in
  validate_generalized_cmh.py):
    CMH statistic = 92.0864, dof = 3, p = 7.805e-20

  All combination methods agree: the two matrices differ overall.
  Fisher's method and Stouffer's method combine independent p-values;
  CMH combines the underlying counts directly and typically has more
  power when, as here, the direction of the difference is largely
  consistent across columns (see figure 03_row_consistency.png).
